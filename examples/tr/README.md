As a prerequisite, terms for the target language must be added to the term files `*.{json,tsv}` in [terms/](terms/) and proofread. In particular, the TSV generated by `trtools term extract/translate` can retain slash-separated candidate lists, mistranslations, source-language leakage, and typos, so it should be reviewed rather than used as-is. Proper nouns common to all topics are fixed in [terms/common.tsv](terms/common.tsv).

- [terms/](terms/): term translations

Each set manages its own target languages separately, and all of them are evaluated by TypeSafe's Jev, so their scores are on one scale. Each set's earlier scores from qwen3.6 (median of three runs) are kept beside Jev's (`SCORES.txt` beside `SCORES-jev.txt`) as the previous evaluator's record. The `make` command can run these sets in sequence.

- [core/](core/): translation of core languages (fr, es, de, ja, zh) (gemma4)
- [onde/](onde/): translation by each model, including additional languages
  - [gemma4/](onde/gemma4/): the baseline verification set

  - [qwen3.6/](onde/qwen3.6/): includes past experiments (comparing self-evaluation with third-party evaluation (gpt-oss) to verify the asymmetry between an LLM's reading and writing ability)

In the momentum topic, Spanish, French and Chinese score lower than in the other topics. The main cause is dropped speaker labels, mostly on short lines such as the back-channel "Oh?" or "Sure.": the label is left out on about a third of the lines in Spanish and French and on fewer lines in Chinese. Jev deducts for that under information completeness, which with fluency is their lowest criterion. German and Japanese keep every label. See [core/README.md](core/README.md) for details.

## Background on Model Selection and Verification

- **Purpose of additional verification**: gemma4's results showed unusually low scores for some languages. Based on this, sets were created to verify whether switching the translation model to gpt-oss or the qwen family improves translation quality.
- **Unifying the evaluation model**: For relative quality comparison, the most important thing is to keep the scoring criteria (the ruler) consistent across all sets. For this reason, every set is scored by the same evaluator. That evaluator was qwen3.6 (median of three runs, including self-evaluation) until it was replaced by TypeSafe's Jev, which reads each criterion as a distribution over five severity levels and varies so little between runs that one run per language is enough. [experimental/13](../../experimental/13/README.md) is how it was chosen and [onde/JEV.md](onde/JEV.md) the run over the corpus.
- **Pinning the evaluator's version**: Jev is pinned to `jev-1.14.0` (`DEFAULT_MODEL` in [trtools/jev.py](../../trtools/jev.py)) rather than an alias; it replaced `jev-1.13.0` on 2026-10-17 ([onde/JEV-1.14.md](onde/JEV-1.14.md)). Changing it changes every score, and `trtools jev` refuses to append to a file written by another version, so the previous version's files are first moved into a `jev-<version>/` directory beside them, where they stay frozen. It then means regenerating every set's Jev output — onde's `jev.jsonl`, `SCORES-jev.txt` and `TREND-jev.jsonl`, core/'s and fr/'s `jev-{topic}.jsonl` and `SCORES-jev.txt`, and the reference translations' `jev-{topic}-{src}.jsonl` and `SCORES-jev.txt` in [examples/evals/](../evals/README.md) — and updating the evaluation model named in each model's `README.md`, in core/'s and fr/'s and in examples/evals/'s, and the per-language table in the top-level [README.md](../../README.md), in the same pass.

### Trend Differences Among Google Models

`gemma4`, `gemma4-31b`, `gemma4-12b`, `gemma4-e4b`, `gemma4-e2b`, `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, `gemini-2.5-flash`, `gemini-3-flash`, and `gemini-3.7-flash` are all made by Google, but their strengths and weaknesses differed by model.

- The per-language scores of the Gemini models correlate only weakly to moderately with each other and with the gemma4 models (Spearman about +0.2 to +0.5) — you can't say trends are similar just because they're from the same vendor. The exception is the gemma4 variants, which track each other closely
- Languages where `gemma4` clearly outperforms `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` are medium-to-high-resource languages where `gemma4` is stable, while `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` is prone to breaking down there due to other-language contamination or source-text leftovers
- Conversely, languages where `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` clearly outperforms overlap with the band where `gemma4` catastrophically breaks down due to code-switching and the like: Basque, the Celtic and Baltic languages, Estonian
- Failure patterns also differ: `gemma4` has localized intrusions of irrelevant words into sentences, as seen with Hungarian, whereas `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` has multiple different writing systems mixed in together for specific languages (e.g. other South Asian language scripts intruding broadly into Sinhala) — the very nature of the breakdown is different
- Of `gemma4`, `gemma4-31b`, `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, and `gemini-2.5-flash`, `gemini-2.5-flash` has the shallowest failure floor: no language breaks down, where the two gemma4 variants collapse on Irish, Welsh, and Basque
- There's no clear superiority between the MoE (26B-A4B) and dense (31B) variants of the same `gemma4`: their means are nearly even and their per-language scores move together, with the dense version winning somewhat more languages head to head
- `gemini-2.5-flash` and `gemini-3-flash` both drop speaker labels in almost every language, and the evaluator deducts for it in each, so both sit in a narrow band below the models that keep their labels; `gemini-3-flash` adds non-natural-language failures of its own (below)
- Among the smaller gemma4 variants, size doesn't order the scores: `gemma4-e4b` beats `gemma4-12b` in most languages (Wilcoxon p < 0.001), as the 12B build scatters East Asian fragments into its output (below), and `gemma4-e2b` falls below both by dropping speaker labels. Every variant, from `gemma4-e2b` up to `gemma4-31b`, collapses on the same low-resource languages (Irish, Welsh, Basque, the Baltic languages, Estonian), so that weakness belongs to the family rather than to one size
- `gemini-3.7-flash` is by far the strongest Google model in this comparison, and even its low-resource floor stays ordinary rather than the collapses seen in the gemma4 variants
- Even within the same Google lineage, differences in model scale, architecture, and tuning policy appear to have a much bigger effect on translation tendencies

### Notable Failure Patterns

- **Cross-model gap on Interlingua**: for a constructed language with thin training resources, differences in pretraining between models tend to show up extremely sharply, splitting clearly into models that maintain a readable range and models that break down.
- **gemma4's peculiar breakdown on Hungarian**: `gemma4`, which showed excellent stability on other low-resource languages, had a peculiar failure only on Hungarian, where unrelated languages (English, Greek, Sinhala, Vietnamese, Russian, etc.) got mixed into the middle of sentences. This suggests that Hungarian's vector placement is uniquely prone to extreme cross-talk with other languages specifically within this model's latent space.
- **gemini-3-flash's non-natural-language contamination failures**: while other models' failures stay at the natural-language level — multilingual contamination, speaker-tag dropout — `gemini-3-flash` showed failure patterns not seen in other models, such as raw JSON fragments leaking into the body text for Slovak, or falling into a loop that keeps generating HTML tags for Lao.
- **gpt-5.6-terra's chain-of-thought leakage**: across a wide range of medium-to-low-resource languages (French, Dutch, Croatian, Swedish, Polish, and many more), `gpt-5.6-terra` intermittently leaves raw internal-reasoning fragments — English snippets like `Wait`, `Hmm`, `final?`, or tool-call-like text such as `assistant to=python?` — embedded directly in the translated dialogue, in place of or alongside the intended line. This differs from ordinary multilingual contamination in that the leaked text is not another natural language but the model's own unfiltered deliberation.
- **space-bunny-alpha's scattered contamination**: rather than breaking down a whole language, `space-bunny-alpha` inserts short foreign fragments into otherwise translated lines, mostly Chinese characters (e.g. `Itu adalah平行 yang...` in Malay, `هذا هو正解` in Arabic) along with English and other-language words. In Indonesian and Japanese its own English deliberation also leaks into the body (`We must ensure no meta. Just translate line: ...`, `Suggestion?`, `let's proceed.`), which is the same kind of failure as `gpt-5.6-terra`'s but on far fewer lines. The fragments show up even in high-resource languages, and Japanese, where they appear in over a quarter of its lines, gets its lowest score.
- **gpt-6-luna's stray `providername` token**: in Mongolian, `gpt-6-luna` ends an otherwise normal line with `...тэр дүр зураг...providername`, a bare identifier-like string rather than a natural-language word or a fragment of deliberation. It occurs once in the whole corpus, but it's a kind of leak not seen in the other models.
- **gemma4-12b's East Asian fragment injection**: `gemma4-12b` inserts short Japanese, Korean, or Chinese fragments into otherwise translated sentences, e.g. `das 사실 jeder kennt` (German, where `사실` means "in fact", matching the source), `die要是 Raffinesse` (German), or `կարծしましょう` (Armenian). It appears in about half of its languages, a few lines each, including high-resource German, where the other gemma4 variants stay clean.
- **gemma4-12b stalling during translation**: while translating, `gemma4-12b` often stopped responding partway through, and the run had to be stopped by hand and restarted. None of the other models behaved this way. The scores above come from the completed reruns, so the stalls don't show in them.
- **Speaker labels lost for good in the smallest models**: `gemma4-e2b` and `qwen3.5-4b` keep the speaker labels for the first few lines, then drop one on a short line (for `gemma4-e2b` usually the back-channel `Yes?` on line 5) and never write another for the rest of the text. The result is a script with no attribution on more than 90 of its 99 lines, in most languages for `gemma4-e2b` and nearly all for `qwen3.5-4b`. In the other models a dropped label is mostly an isolated line; here a single drop becomes the pattern the rest of the output follows. `nemotron-3.5-super-vl`, a far larger model, does the same in 14 of its 67 languages, and earlier: the labels stop after line 2, before any short back-channel, and the rest of the script is written as unattributed lines.
- **bonsai2-27b's quantization-induced degeneration**: `bonsai2-27b` is Qwen 3.8 27B quantized to ternary (PTQ1_0), so comparing it with the unquantized model isolates what extreme quantization costs. The damage is not spread evenly: the highest-resource languages hold up best (Chinese, Portuguese, French, Spanish), and the loss concentrates in the middle band that the unquantized model could still handle (Romanian, Arabic, Czech, Indonesian, Slovak, Croatian). In that band the model stops producing a translation at all: for Lao, Burmese, Thai, and Mongolian it falls into character- or phrase-level repetition loops that swell the output to several times the source size, while for Slovak, Slovene, and Swahili the body text vanishes and only the speaker labels are emitted line after line — the same dropout the unquantized model already showed, pushed much further. Finnish came back as the untranslated English source. Unlike the contamination and leakage patterns of other models, these are degeneracies of the decoding process itself rather than wrong-language or wrong-content output, suggesting that quantization erodes the ability to sustain generation in a language before it erodes the knowledge of that language.

- **qwen3.8-flash-next's speaker labels lost partway through**: `qwen3.8-flash-next` keeps the content intact to the end, but in some languages the speaker labels stop and never come back, as in the smallest models. In French, Italian, Persian, and Tamil this happens at the same place, around line 65 of 99 (after the back-channel `Yes...` on line 62); Persian and Tamil had already been dropping the label on short back-channel lines before that, while French and Italian had kept almost every one. In Mongolian the labels stop after line 4, leaving 95 lines without attribution. Since the body text itself stays translated, this costs it scores in languages it otherwise handles well, French most of all.
- **deepseek-v4.1-flash's back-channel overwritten by the previous line**: in 15 of its 67 languages, `deepseek-v4.1-flash` replaces a short back-channel line with a copy of the line before it, speaker label included, so the line count still matches the source but the back-channel is lost and its turn is credited to the wrong speaker. Most cases fall on the same place: Luc's `Yes...` on line 62 becomes a second copy of Camille's line 61, in Spanish, Portuguese, Greek, Persian, Hindi, Vietnamese, and others. Unlike the speaker-label dropout of the smallest models, the labels it drops elsewhere are scattered and come back on the next line.
- **saluki-27b's progressive truncation**: `saluki-27b` is another low-bit quantization of Qwen 3.8 27B (IQ2-mix), and it degenerates differently from `bonsai2-27b`. Its output never bloats: each line starts out translated, but the lines get cut off mid-sentence, shorter and shorter, until from some point on only the speaker name remains, and sometimes only part of it (`Cam` for `Camille`). In Czech only the first four lines are translated in full; in Malayalam, Sinhala, Irish, and Burmese more than 80 of the 99 lines have no body text. This is the unquantized model's dropout pushed to the end, like `bonsai2-27b`'s Slovak and Slovene, but here it is the main failure rather than one of two. The two quantizations score about the same overall yet fail on different languages (Spearman +0.65 per language): `saluki-27b` keeps Indonesian, Korean, and Arabic where `bonsai2-27b` breaks down, and loses Chinese (33.1 against 89.6), which `bonsai2-27b` and the unquantized model both translate well.
- **haiku-5.5's mid-word script switching and leaked editing note**: `haiku-5.5`'s contamination mostly sits inside single words that switch from the target script to Latin partway through: `preispitаме`, `нијansa`, and `барame` in Macedonian, `ფundamentალურ` in Georgian, `Nevероватно` in otherwise Latin-script Serbian, `वर्गसँगalso` in Nepali. In Armenian whole English phrases stand in for the translation (`Դու absolutely right ես`, `բնությունն ասում է՝ stop`). In Bulgarian, line 88 reads `Фascinating... ремонт: Luc: Fascinating -> Удивително е ...`: a half-translated word, then what looks like the model's own correction note (`ремонт`, "repair", and an arrow to the fixed line), left in the body ahead of the actual translation.
- **glm-5.3's draft and revision on the same line**: `glm-5.3` often writes a line and then writes it again on the same line, speaker label included, as if restarting after a first attempt: `Luc: Capisco.Luc: Capisco.` (Italian), `Camille: DobřeCamille: Dobře.` (Czech), `Luc: À, vângLuc: À đúng rồi.` (Vietnamese, the first attempt cut off mid-word), or two different wordings of line 7 one after the other in Tagalog. Short back-channel lines are the most affected, but not the only ones; about 90 lines across 45 languages are written this way. In a few languages the deliberation between the two versions is written out in English: `Wait, let me reconsider. "The famous Abbe limit!" ... Let me think about Romanian grammar.` (Romanian), `Actually, let me reconsider:` and `Hmm, let me reconsider "قابل احترام"` (Urdu), a paragraph justifying the choice of `Понятно...` (Russian), and `- wait, the speaker is Camille here.` (Irish). The line count stays matched to the source, unlike a repeated line; it is closer to `gpt-5.6-terra`'s chain-of-thought leakage, but with the revised translation kept next to the draft rather than replacing it.
- **mai-code-1.1-flash's mid-line switch to English and lines cut off with `?`**: in Hebrew and Georgian, `mai-code-1.1-flash` starts a line in the target language and finishes it in English: `...שמ everyone knows, in fact.` and `...both follow the same rule of the square of the amplitude…` (Hebrew), `როდესაც ვცდილობთ extreemალური სიზუსტით localized wave, i.e., a small width delta x, we lose all information about its direction.` (Georgian). The Georgian switches also bring in Dutch and German fragments (`კეთzlich to`, `natuur says stop`, `extreem`), so the leak is not English alone. In Malayalam and Kannada it fails differently: lines stop mid-sentence and end with a question mark the source doesn't have, sometimes several (`...ഏറ്റവും ആഴത്തിലുള്ള????`), on more than a dozen lines in Malayalam. Burmese, its lowest score, degrades into repeated syllables (`ကိန်းတိန်တိန်ပဲ`) rather than either of these.
- **ornith-1.5's lines replaced by replies to an empty request**: in a few languages `ornith-1.5` writes, in place of a line, what reads as a reply to a request with nothing to translate, rather than a translation. In Irish it translates the first four lines and then writes `Níl aon téacs le aistriú curtha ar fáil...` ("No text to translate has been provided. To follow the instruction, try entering the text and translating it into Irish") on each of the remaining 95 lines, with no speaker label, which leaves its Irish at 6.0. In Danish, line 71 is the English `No response requested.`. In Catalan it goes the other way: Luc's back-channels `Yes...` (line 62) and `Yes.` (line 82) become long monologues credited to Camille that the source doesn't have, the first of them about 1,400 characters, each ending in a question addressed to the listener (`La nostra conversa continua, i la pregunta és: ...`). The line count stays matched to the source in every case.
- **nemotron-3.5-super-vl's script mixing, token salad, and repeated line**: beyond the high-resource languages `nemotron-3.5-super-vl` mixes in other scripts and languages at the level of words and word fragments: `Це абсолютно令人 stunning паралель.` (Ukrainian), `Şunun berühm Heisenberg belirsizlik ilkesidir.` (Turkish, German `berühmt` cut short), `ათასGamma-მ, kicking соң gelsfilter-间аき...` (Georgian), and in the speaker labels themselves, `ΚαμILED` for Camille in Greek and `ল্যukin` for Luc in Bengali. Burmese is written entirely in Latin letters, a romanization mixed with English (`Mingalaba everyone, pyro "Bridges in Physics" ko ...`). In Armenian, line 81 becomes about 1,800 characters of token salad, fragments of Korean, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Spanish, Kannada, and more, together with a chat-template control token `<|constrain|>` (`{//{ 염 لذلك aprendizaje说到这里՝ ...`). In Khmer one line is written 13 times in a row in place of lines 18–30, the only repetition loop in its output; the line count still matches the source.

No universal translation model exists, strongly suggesting the importance of choosing a model to match the language.

## Scope of the Comparison

What the comparison above can and cannot be read as. These were carried over from [experimental/13/PORT.md](../../experimental/13/PORT.md) section 8 when experiments 13 and 14 were frozen; this section is where they are kept up to date.

- **One source text, chosen to be hard.** Every onde score is a translation of one document, [onde-en.txt](../onde-en.txt). It is deliberately spoken in form and technical in content, a combination that is hard to translate, so that translators differ. The scores are therefore not a general-purpose measure of multilingual ability, and whether the ranking would hold for another genre is untested. This is accepted as the corpus's design rather than treated as a gap to fill with a second source.
- **The top three are tied; `gpt-6-sol`, `gpt-6.1-sol`, and `gpt-6-astra` lead them.** `gpt-5.6-luna`, `union-alpha`, and `gpt-6-luna` are not separated per language by the paired test on the per-language differences (Wilcoxon p = 0.44 between `gpt-5.6-luna` and `union-alpha`, 0.46 between `gpt-6-luna` and `gpt-5.6-luna`, 0.65 between `gpt-6-luna` and `union-alpha`); `gpt-5.6-luna` and `union-alpha` were not separated by qwen3.6 either ([experimental/13/REPORT.md](../../experimental/13/REPORT.md) section 2), so the three are treated as tied. All three are separated from `ox-alpha` and `gemini-3.7-flash` (p < 0.001). The tie is in overall level only: per language `gpt-6-luna` and `gpt-5.6-luna` correlate only moderately (Spearman +0.60), trading wins across languages. The small differences in their means are not read as an order, and nothing further is being tried to separate them. `union-alpha` turned out to be a router that calls a stronger model when a task needs one, so the tie is between single models and a system of several. `gpt-6.1-sol`, scored only by Jev, has the narrowest spread of any model and is 0.9 to 1.5 points above the three on average. The paired test separates it from `gpt-5.6-luna` (p = 0.001, 43 languages won to 22) and `union-alpha` (p = 0.010, 45 to 22), but only marginally from `gpt-6-luna` (p = 0.051, 37 to 28), and its per-language correlation with them is moderate to high (Spearman +0.68 to +0.79). It is therefore read as ahead of them, though not a tier apart from `gpt-6-luna`. Under `jev-1.13.0` it was separated from the three only marginally (p = 0.017 to 0.055); the lead firmed up with the evaluator version, not with any change in the translations ([onde/JEV-1.14.md](onde/JEV-1.14.md)). `gpt-6-astra`, also scored only by Jev, comes closest: it is not separated from `gpt-6.1-sol` (p = 0.41, 30 to 36) and tracks it per language more closely than any other model does (Spearman +0.86), and like it is separated from `gpt-5.6-luna` (p = 0.013) and `union-alpha` (p = 0.039) but not from `gpt-6-luna` (p = 0.32). `gpt-6-sol`, also scored only by Jev, has the highest mean and median of any model, but only 0.10 above `gpt-6.1-sol` in mean: the paired test does not separate it from `gpt-6.1-sol` (p = 0.38, 37 to 29) and only marginally from `gpt-6-astra` (p = 0.068, 40 to 24), with per-language correlation of +0.79 and +0.83. It is the only one of the three that is also separated from `gpt-6-luna` (p = 0.010, 41 to 26), besides `gpt-5.6-luna` (p < 0.001, 47 to 19) and `union-alpha` (p = 0.003, 44 to 22). The three are therefore read as one leading group with no settled order among them.
- **Greek and Interlingua sit low for every model, likely because Jev knows them less well.** Among the ten models whose mean is 80 or above (`gpt-6-sol`, `gpt-6.1-sol`, `gpt-6-astra`, `union-alpha`, `gpt-5.6-luna`, `gpt-6-luna`, `ox-alpha`, `gemini-3.7-flash`, `haiku-5.5`, `qwen3.8-flash-next`), Greek is the third-lowest language on average and Interlingua the fourth, and in most models both rank in the bottom dozen of 67 languages (Greek in 17 of 35, Interlingua in 21). Greek is a medium-resource language, and the ten models' Greek translations show no breakdown (every speaker label kept but for one line in one model, no other-language text beyond names and one English "stop"), yet Jev rates every criterion uniformly lower than for comparable languages. Over the ten models above, its mean confidence is 0.45 on Interlingua and 0.52 on Greek, against 0.54 on Irish and 0.69 on Bulgarian. ## Evaluation Design Policy: Why Speaker Tags Aren't Separated

In this verification, dialogue script speaker tags (like `Camille:` or `Luc:`) are currently not separated from the translation target and appended afterward.

In the past, a design was also tried where the translation target was limited to the body text only, and speaker tags were appended afterward by the logic side. With this approach, it's easier to avoid deductions from tag dropout, and measure only the translation quality of the body text itself.

However, that would exclude from evaluation an ability that matters in real use: whether the model can maintain the structure of a long dialogue script all the way through. Speaker-tag dropout isn't just a superficial formatting mistake — it can be a manifestation of insufficient attention and stability in sustaining structure over a long output.

Also, treating only speaker tags as a protected exception blurs the boundary with line dropout, sentence dropout, and correspondence breakdowns that can occur in ordinary body text. In real-world use, omissions and mix-ups can occur not just in tags but in the body text itself, so treating tags as a special case risks overestimating a model's reliability.

For this reason, the comparisons in this README treat translation quality as including not just "semantic accuracy" but also "whether the whole script can be output without breaking." Jev deducts for a dropped or swapped speaker tag under information completeness, consistently from one translation to the next, where qwen3.6 as evaluator did not register it ([experimental/13/REPORT.md](../../experimental/13/REPORT.md) section 4). So models like `gpt-oss`, `gemini-2.5-flash`, and `gemini-3-flash`, whose content is often good but which drop tags in almost every language, sit lower than their content alone would place them; this is recorded as part of the practical robustness difference.

## Observations and Discussion: Asymmetry Between an LLM's Reading and Writing Ability

During this verification process, while qwen3.6 was the evaluator, a striking asymmetry was observed between an LLM's "evaluation ability (reading)" and "translation ability (writing)." This phenomenon resembles the gap between receptive and productive ability in human foreign-language learning (being able to read and understand something is not the same as being able to write it correctly).

**qwen3.6's case**:
- As an evaluator, it accurately pointed out grammatical errors and unnatural expressions, delivering a coherent, sound assessment.
- As a translator, it produced low-quality output for languages like Hindi, with broken grammar and frequent spelling mistakes (immature writing ability).

### Comparative Verification: Self-Evaluation vs. Third-Party Evaluation

Initially, to evaluate translations generated by qwen3.6 while avoiding self-evaluation bias, a different model (gpt-oss) was tried as the evaluator. The results revealed the following.

1. **Absence of self-evaluation bias**: even when made to evaluate its own broken text (self-evaluation), qwen3.6 did not score leniently, and delivered an objective, extremely strict assessment.
2. **Difference in scoring criteria**: both models functioned equally well at "the ability to accurately detect and point out errors such as prompt contamination or mistranslation (reading ability)." However, there was an extreme difference in their **scoring criteria** for those errors. gpt-oss generously gave high scores as long as the meaning came through, whereas qwen3.6 mercilessly deducted points for grammatical breakdowns.

These results showed that an LLM's self-evaluation isn't necessarily strongly biased, and that the reading/writing asymmetry shows up starkly instead. And because how lenient a model's scoring is toward errors differs fundamentally from model to model, this became strong evidence that, for relative quality comparison, "unifying the scoring criteria by using the same evaluation model (the ruler)" matters far more than "avoiding self-evaluation bias."

### Behavioral Differences from Execution Environment (Provider) Differences

The verification in [gpt-oss/](onde/gpt-oss/) compared the same models (qwen3.6, gpt-oss) across independent pipelines on local (Ollama) and cloud API (OpenRouter). This yielded the following important operational insights.

1. **Error-detection ability is environment-independent**: even with different providers or different quantization, the evaluation model's ability to detect and point out bugs (garbled text, prompt leakage, etc.) in the text matched completely.
2. **Translation-generation hallucinations are environment-independent**: critical bugs caused by gpt-oss when translating minor languages, such as "other-language contamination" or "system-prompt leakage," occurred in common on both local and cloud. This is caused by a limitation intrinsic to the model's ability (immature writing ability).
3. **How structured-output (JSON) bugs manifest is environment-dependent**: on the cloud (OpenRouter), cases where a specific JSON field got a meaningless value and broke the output stood out, whereas on local (Ollama), cases of falling into an infinite loop stood out (this one is recoverable via the tool's automatic retry).

To build a fully automated pipeline, it is essential to fix the "ruler" to include not just the model but also the execution environment (provider), and to have error handling that accounts for each provider's quirks in how hallucinations manifest.
